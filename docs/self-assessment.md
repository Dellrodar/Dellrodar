Completing my Computer Science degree and building this ePortfolio represents more than finishing an academic program. I originally entered the program while working in retail, balancing two jobs, raising a family, and trying to build a different future for myself. For the first two years, I was able to maintain that balance, but eventually the workload became too much and my grades began to suffer. I decided to step away from school, expecting the break to be temporary. Instead, ten years passed before I had the opportunity to return and finish what I had started.

By the time I returned, my life and career had changed considerably. I had entered the technology industry and was working as a front-end developer, but shortly afterward the company I worked for went out of business and I lost my position. I decided that completing my degree and continuing to grow professionally were goals I was no longer willing to leave unfinished. After several months of focused study and job searching, I moved into full-stack development and continued progressing both academically and professionally. Returning with industry experience made portions of the coursework easier, but it also exposed gaps in my knowledge that I had not realized were there.

Throughout the program, I expanded beyond application development into areas such as DevOps, security, databases, architecture, testing, and development tooling. More importantly, I learned to view technical problems from multiple perspectives. Today, I am more comfortable working under pressure, meeting deadlines, evaluating competing solutions, and making technical trade-offs when there is no single obvious answer. Developing this ePortfolio has given me an opportunity to bring those skills together and demonstrate not only that I can build software, but that I can evaluate why a solution should be built a particular way and communicate those decisions to others. That combination of academic development and professional experience has prepared me to contribute across more of the software development lifecycle and has expanded what I can offer an engineering organization.

## Collaborating in a Team Environment

Collaboration has become a significant part of my professional role. I currently lead two teams of developers, which includes assigning and coordinating work, reviewing code, mentoring developers, making architectural decisions, coordinating releases, and guiding technical discussions. My responsibilities also extend beyond development itself. I communicate directly with stakeholders, evaluate new technologies, contribute to business development, and participate in hiring and technical interviews. These experiences have taught me that leading a development team is not simply about having the strongest technical opinion. It requires creating an environment where different experiences and perspectives can contribute to a better solution.

One example came from an earlier team where another engineer, Henry, and I disagreed about the structure of an API and some of the technologies that should support it. Neither approach was entirely wrong, and rather than treating the decision as one person winning the argument, we worked through the advantages and disadvantages of both. The resulting design incorporated parts of each approach and ultimately improved the application. Experiences like this have changed how I approach technical disagreement. A strong engineering decision often comes from understanding why someone favors a different solution and determining whether those ideas can address weaknesses in your own.

Code reviews and mentoring have reinforced that same lesson. When I review code, functionality is only one consideration. I also evaluate downstream effects, security posture, potential vulnerabilities, coding standards, maintainability, and how a change fits into the larger system. Mentoring junior developers has also forced me to improve the way I communicate. Explaining the same concept to different people often requires different examples or approaches, and I frequently learn something myself in the process. That experience has also changed how I document my own work. Earlier in my career, comments and documentation were often secondary concerns. Today, I consistently document decisions, implementation details, and the reasoning behind them throughout the development lifecycle because I have seen how much that context matters to the people who maintain a system later.

## Communicating with Stakeholders

Effective technical communication depends heavily on understanding the audience. In my professional work, I communicate with engineers, project leadership, civilian stakeholders, government stakeholders, and other members of management. The same technical issue cannot always be explained in the same way to each group. When communicating with leadership or stakeholders, I generally keep updates concise and focus on impact, risk, cost, and the decisions that need to be made, providing deeper technical detail when it is useful or requested. With engineers, conversations can go much further into implementation details and the reasoning behind a proposed solution.

A recent database transition illustrates that distinction. I needed to explain why moving from document-based indexing storage to PostgreSQL was a better architectural and financial decision while also demonstrating that the transition would preserve the application's existing capabilities. For a nontechnical audience, detailing database internals would not have helped make the decision clearer. Instead, I kept the technical explanation high level and used real-world analogies to explain how the data would be organized differently, why that approach better fit our needs, and how it could reduce costs without sacrificing functionality.

Communication also means being willing to challenge a proposed direction when important questions remain unanswered. In a current project, I have pushed back on a requested feature because the application does not presently contain the information necessary to support it and several requirements remain unresolved. Rather than implementing something simply because it was requested, I believe part of my responsibility as an engineer is to identify those gaps before they become architectural or maintenance problems.

Documentation supports that communication long after a meeting has ended. Professionally, I produce architecture diagrams, API documentation, ADRs for major technical decisions and recommendations, Confluence documentation for releases and project objectives, README files for repositories, Jira tickets, technical proposals for business development and pilot programs, technical knowledge-base articles, and support documentation for users and help desks. Producing documentation for such different audiences has reinforced that technical communication is not simply about being accurate. It also has to provide the right level of information for the person using it.

Interestingly, I sometimes find explaining a complex system to another engineer more difficult than explaining it to a nontechnical stakeholder. Engineers naturally want to understand how and why each component works, but differences in experience and specialization mean that seemingly familiar concepts may not be equally familiar to everyone. That curiosity is valuable, but it can also lead a discussion into details that are not relevant to the immediate decision. Learning when to explore those questions and when to redirect a conversation toward the problem being solved has become an important part of how I communicate and lead technical discussions.

## Data Structures and Algorithms

My approach to algorithmic problem-solving has changed considerably over the course of the program. Earlier in my development experience, I often started implementing a solution before forming a complete plan. I would experiment, get something working, and then use that initial implementation as the foundation for the rest of the solution. That approach can still be useful for prototyping, but I now spend more time understanding the problem, researching possible approaches, and identifying a reasonable path before committing to an implementation. This has helped me make more deliberate decisions instead of allowing the first working solution to become the final design by default.

Scalability has become one of the largest factors in how I evaluate a solution, but it cannot be considered independently from complexity, data structures, maintainability, or performance. In practice, engineering often requires finding an acceptable trade-off between those concerns. I have applied this reasoning both academically and professionally. In the capstone, replacing rigid exact matching with similarity-based evaluation allowed the application to consider partial matches rather than treating every result as a simple match or nonmatch. Professionally, I have had to make similar decisions about how data organization affects scalability, query performance, and the capabilities available to users.

One example involved migrating away from a document-oriented indexing model toward a relational data model. The previous structure supported search effectively, but it limited how we could relate and aggregate information. Redesigning the data around relational structures improved retrieval performance for our use case and gave us access to aggregate operations that were difficult or unavailable in the previous design. Those changes improved both the underlying system and the information we could provide to users. Experiences like this have reinforced that selecting a data structure or algorithm is not an isolated programming decision. It can affect scalability, available functionality, system performance, and ultimately the user experience.

## Software Engineering and Databases

My growth in software engineering has not come from a single practice or technology. Over the course of the degree, I have become more deliberate about architecture, testing, CI/CD, database design, documentation, and security. Many of these practices influence one another. Testing is easier when responsibilities are clearly separated, CI/CD is more reliable when projects have consistent structure and automation, and security is more effective when it is considered early in the development process rather than added after implementation. Professionally, this has also changed the way I think about maintainability. Technical debt can accumulate quickly, and decisions that save time in the short term can create significantly more work later if their long-term effects are not considered.

One of the largest changes in how I approach software design is my focus on separation of concerns. Earlier projects often placed responsibilities closer together because the immediate goal was simply to produce a working application. I now place much greater value on clear boundaries between presentation, application logic, APIs, and persistence. This makes individual components easier to test, maintain, replace, and extend without creating unnecessary dependencies throughout the system.

The capstone gave me an opportunity to apply that approach by separating the original application into a React frontend, FastAPI backend, and PostgreSQL database. These technologies were not selected only because they were more modern. FastAPI provides strong API support and automatically generated documentation, React provides a flexible component-based frontend and a large ecosystem, and PostgreSQL provides a mature relational foundation that can be extended when additional functionality is needed. In this project, for example, `pg_trgm` supports similarity matching without requiring another external service.

My understanding of database design has also changed significantly. Earlier in my development experience, it was easy to think of the database primarily as the place where an application stored and retrieved information. I now view the database as an important part of the application's overall design. Schema structure, normalization, constraints, indexing, migrations, and data integrity all influence how efficiently and reliably an application can operate. Even a seemingly small decision, such as whether a frequently queried column should be indexed, can have a substantial effect as the amount of data and number of users increase. A well-designed database does more than preserve information. It provides structure, enforces expectations about the data, and supports the application in retrieving and using that information efficiently.

## Security

My approach to security has changed significantly since I first began programming. Early on, I thought of security as something that was added near the end of development after the main functionality was complete. Through both coursework and professional experience, I learned that this approach creates unnecessary risk. I now try to shift security left by considering it during design and carrying those decisions throughout implementation, testing, deployment, and maintenance.

Professional experience has been especially important in developing that mindset. On one project, I worked with systems containing sensitive information that contractors were not permitted to access directly. That requirement changed the way I thought about application design because security could not rely on a single permission check or control. Practices such as encryption with KMS-managed keys, secrets management, automated code scanning, protection against prompt injection, and other defense-in-depth measures became essential parts of the architecture. The objective was not simply to prevent an external attacker from gaining access, but to design the system so that users and services could access only the information required to perform their responsibilities.

The principle of least privilege is now one of the first considerations I make when designing a feature. I determine which systems, data, and operations the feature actually needs and avoid granting broader access for convenience. I also assume that an individual safeguard could eventually fail, which makes defense in depth equally important. Multiple independent controls reduce the likelihood that the failure of one mechanism will compromise the entire application.

The capstone applies this broader security mindset through several complementary decisions rather than a single security feature. Role-based access control limits users to appropriate actions, authentication and password protections provide controlled access to the system, backend validation prevents the client from becoming the application's security boundary, and safe error handling reduces unnecessary exposure of internal information. Preserving records rather than relying entirely on destructive deletion also supports auditability and data integrity. Together, these measures reflect how I now approach security: as a system-wide responsibility intended to protect confidentiality, integrity, privacy, and reliability. A strong security strategy does more than defend against attackers. When incorporated correctly, it strengthens the application as a whole.

## Closing Reflection

Completing this degree is something I am proud of for reasons that go beyond academics. I wanted to prove to myself and to my family that I could return after a ten-year break and finish what I had started. Balancing a full-time career, full-time coursework, and the responsibilities of being a parent has been one of the most demanding challenges of my life. Finishing the program required discipline, persistence, and the ability to keep moving forward when my time and attention were divided across competing priorities.

At the same time, completing the degree has reinforced how much more there is for me to learn. Cybersecurity and artificial intelligence are two areas where I already have professional exposure, but I want to continue developing deeper technical knowledge in both. I plan to continue taking courses, pursuing certifications, and expanding my experience outside of formal degree requirements. The pace of change in computer science makes it difficult to predict exactly where my career will lead, which is why I do not want to define myself too narrowly around a single technology or specialization.

Over the next several years, I want to continue developing as both an engineer and a technical leader. I enjoy mentoring developers, helping teams make better technical decisions, and creating environments where others can grow. Whether that eventually leads further into engineering management, architecture, security, artificial intelligence, or another area, I want to maintain the flexibility to adapt as the industry evolves.

If I could speak to the version of myself who first entered this program, I think he would be most surprised by the level of dedication I now bring to both my career and education. I have grown not only technically, but also in patience, discipline, emotional maturity, and mental resilience. Those qualities have become just as important to my success as the technical skills represented in this portfolio. This degree represents the completion of a goal I once left unfinished, but it also marks the beginning of the next stage of my professional development.

## How the Artifacts Fit Together

The three enhancements that follow use the same CS 340 animal shelter dashboard to demonstrate different areas of my development as a computer scientist. Using one artifact allowed me to show how software engineering, algorithms, databases, and security influence one another within a complete system rather than presenting those skills as isolated exercises.

The [software design enhancement](https://dellrodar.github.io/Dellrodar/enhancement-one/) restructures the original Dash application into a more maintainable full-stack architecture with clearer separation between the frontend, backend, and data layers. The [algorithms enhancement](https://dellrodar.github.io/Dellrodar/enhancement-two/) replaces rigid rescue filtering with a more flexible similarity-based matching process that supports scoring, ranking, and more useful search results. The [database enhancement](https://dellrodar.github.io/Dellrodar/enhancement-three/) introduces a normalized PostgreSQL data layer with migrations, constraints, indexing, and additional support for the application's matching functionality.

Together, these enhancements show the progression of an earlier academic project into a system designed using the engineering practices and technical judgment I have developed throughout the Computer Science program. They demonstrate the five course outcomes across one coherent application while showing the relationship between architecture, algorithms, data, security, and user needs. The enhancement narratives and supporting artifacts that follow provide the technical evidence behind the skills and professional growth described in this self-assessment.
